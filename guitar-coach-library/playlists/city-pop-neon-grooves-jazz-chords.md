## What City Pop Actually Is

To understand City Pop, we have to look past the neon-tinted anime aesthetics and dive into the harmonic and economic engines that built it. Emerging in the mid-to-late 1970s and peaking in the mid-1980s, City Pop was the soundtrack of Japan's post-war economic miracle and subsequent bubble era. Flush with capital, Japanese record labels gave artists unlimited studio budgets, letting them hire the finest session players and import state-of-the-art synthesizers and recording gear from the US. The result was a hyper-sophisticated, pristine hybrid of American West Coast AOR (Adult Oriented Rock), jazz-fusion, funk, disco, and Brazilian bossa nova, all filtered through a distinct Japanese lyrical sensibility centered on urban romance, coastal leisure, and bittersweet nostalgia (*mono no aware*).

Harmonically, City Pop represents a massive leap in sophistication from standard Western three-chord pop. Rather than simple diatonic progressions, City Pop leans heavily on jazz theory. It is characterized by the frequent use of **major 9th and minor 9th chords**, **half-diminished ii-V-I turnarounds**, **secondary dominants**, and **slash chords** (like $IV/V$ or $bVII/I$—think $F/G$ or $Bb/C$ acting as suspended dominant 11th extensions). 

A classic City Pop harmonic staple is the **"Royal Road" progression** ($IV\Delta7 - V/IV - iii7 - vi7$, or sometimes $IV\Delta7 - III7 - vi7 - I7$), which provides an endlessly looping, bittersweet tension that never quite resolves to a stable tonic $I$ chord. For someone with your background in voice leading and a cappella arranging, this is a goldmine: the inner voices of these guitar parts move in highly logical, step-wise motions, shifting by only a half or whole step to outline complex jazz extensions while the bass line supplies the syncopated root movement.

Rhythmically and texturally, the guitar in City Pop is the ultimate pocket-keeper. Unlike American rock of the era, which relied on heavy power chords and distorted riffs, City Pop guitarists favored ultra-clean, compressed, and chorused tones. The rhythm playing is highly syncopated, utilizing 16th-note funk scratching, double-stop comping (highly influenced by Nile Rodgers of Chic), and interlocking dual-guitar arrangements where one guitar chugs on a low-register groove while the other sprinkles high-register chord stabs. This tight, clean rhythm style directly laid the groundwork for 1990s Shibuya-kei and, eventually, modern Japanese math rock and J-pop. Modern Japanese math rock (e.g., *Toe*, *Lite*, *Covet*) inherits this exact lineage: instead of the raw, dissonant odd-time signatures of Western math rock, it features hyper-clean, fluid, tapping-heavy jazz-fusion chord voicings, sweet melodies, and intense syncopated rhythm coordination.

## The Playlist: Perspective and Arc

This curriculum is structured as both a **chronological timeline** and a **geographical map**, tracking how the sophisticated guitar language of Tokyo's bubble era traveled across the globe and evolved into the modern indie, J-pop, and math-rock crossovers of today. We prioritize recordings where the guitar is either the primary vehicle for the groove or the emotional voice piece of the arrangement, giving you a comprehensive vocabulary-building guide for clean tones, syncopated comping, and fusion phrasing.

The playlist is divided into four distinct acts:
1. **The Foundations & Roots (1976–1979):** The acoustic warmth, West Coast AOR influence, and early jazz-fusion integration of the late 70s.
2. **The Neon-Lit Golden Era (1980–1984):** The absolute peak of bubble-era sophistication, featuring legendary session guitarists like Kenji Omura, Tsunehide Matsuki, and Issei Noro delivering pristine rhythm tracks and soaring, chorused fusion solos.
3. **The Acoustic & Sophisticated Continuation (2000s):** How the chordal sophistication of the 80s was kept alive by modern indie-pop masterminds.
4. **The Global Echoes & Modern Revival (2016–Present):** The modern international response, showcasing how artists in Taiwan, Thailand, South Korea, the UK, and the US have adapted the City Pop guitar language to bedroom-pop, modern R&B, and math-pop styles.

## Track-by-Track Commentary

### Act I: The Foundations & Roots (1976–1979)

#### 1. LADY PINK PANTHER — Shigeru Suzuki (*LAGOON*, 1976)
*Listening Cue:* Suzuki was the guitarist for the legendary band Happy End and is the godfather of City Pop guitar. In this track, listen to the West Coast-inspired, laid-back acoustic and electric rhythm guitar blending. Suzuki uses smooth major 9th slide entries and a beautiful clean single-note fill language that bridges folk-rock and early City Pop. Pay attention to how he uses sliding double-stops to transition between chords.

#### 2. Tokai — Taeko Onuki (*SUNSHOWER*, 1977)
*Listening Cue:* Focus on Tsunehide Matsuki's rhythm guitar work, which is a masterclass in sparse, funky comping. Matsuki plays a highly syncopated funk-scratch pattern that sits perfectly behind Ryuichi Sakamoto's Fender Rhodes, utilizing tight voicing shifts on the top three strings. His playing is incredibly dynamic, expanding during the choruses to lock in with the horn section's staccato bursts.

#### 3. ノスタルジア・オブ・アイランド — Tatsuro Yamashita (*PACIFIC*, 1978)
*Listening Cue:* Tatsuro Yamashita is legendary for his solo rhythm guitar playing, but since his solo catalog is famously absent from Spotify, this rare instrumental collaboration from the album *PACIFIC* is a must-listen. Here, he plays a gorgeous, breezy acoustic and electric guitar track. Listen to the voice-leading of the dominant 9th and major 7th chord shifts that establish a tropical resort vibe. The nylon-string soloing has a distinct bossa nova influence.

#### 4. My Life — Noriko Miyamoto & Isao Suzuki (*Push*, 1978)
*Listening Cue:* Backed by the legendary jazz bassist Isao Suzuki and fusion elite, listen to Kenji Omura's incredibly sophisticated jazz chords and smooth, horn-like guitar fills. His phrasing uses wide interval jumps, reminiscent of alto sax lines, sliding elegantly into the chord alterations. The guitar solo is exceptionally lyrical, demonstrating how to play jazz lines over a tight, modern soul groove.

#### 5. BLUE LAGOON — Masayoshi Takanaka (*JOLLY JIVE*, 1979)
*Listening Cue:* Takanaka is the absolute king of Japanese summer-fusion-guitar. Focus on his compressed, singing lead guitar tone (achieved via a Yamaha SG) and the joyous, highly melodic pentatonic lead line that outlines the chord changes. The rhythm track is a masterclass in driving, Latin-fusion strumming, using fast, clean 16th-note strum patterns to maintain high rhythmic energy.

### Act II: The Neon-Lit Golden Era (1980–1984)

#### 6. Mayonaka no Door~stay with me — Miki Matsubara (*Pocket Park (Remastered)*, 1979)
*Listening Cue:* The global anthem of City Pop. The opening features a legendary muted guitar scratching and subsequent slick jazz-fusion chord stabs (using minor 9ths and dominant 13ths). Notice how the rhythm guitar acts as a rhythmic engine, answering the brass lines with brief, clean, single-note commentary, and keeping the verses tight by playing only on the offbeats.

#### 7. テレフォン・ナンバー — Junko Ohashi (*Tea For Tears*, 1981)
*Listening Cue:* Focus on the incredibly tight, highly-syncopated funk-chug rhythm guitar. The guitarist plays highly rhythmic double-stops and muted 16th-note scratches, acting almost like a second percussion instrument behind the slap bass. The voice leading in the chorus is remarkably smooth, utilizing minor 7th to dominant 9th shifts with minimal physical hand movements.

#### 8. Fly-By-Day — Toshiki Kadomatsu (*Crate Diggers: Japanese City Pop Vol. 1*, 1981)
*Listening Cue:* Toshiki Kadomatsu's trademark "resort funk" sound. Listen to the sparkling, highly-compressed clean rhythm guitar stabs on the upbeats (beats 2 and 4), keeping the pocket tight and allowing the synth bass to breathe. This track shows how to use simple triadic voicings on the top three strings to cut through a dense synthesizer arrangement.

#### 9. Plastic Love — Mariya Takeuchi (*Expressions*, 1984)
*Listening Cue:* The absolute masterpiece of Tokyo's bubble era. Listen to the interlocking electric guitar parts: one playing a continuous funk-chugging 16th-note pattern on the left ear, while a second guitar adds sweet, clean fills and chorused double-stops on the right. During the choruses, the guitars play beautiful voice-led jazzy chord extensions that emphasize the bittersweet melody.

#### 10. 朝焼け - Live at Chuo Kaikan Hall, Tokyo, Feb. 1982 — CASIOPEA (*MINT JAMS(Live)*, 1982)
*Listening Cue:* Issei Noro's absolute masterpiece. The opening clean guitar riff is one of the most famous in Japanese music history. Listen to how Noro locks in with Tetsuo Sakurai's legendary slap bass, using extremely precise, dry single-note picking and flawless chord stabs. This is the bridge between City Pop and J-Fusion; the guitar is the ultimate rhythmic and melodic driving force.

#### 11. SAY GOODBYE — Hiroshi Sato (*Awakening*, 1982)
*Listening Cue:* Underneath Sato's lush LinnDrum and synth-pop arrangements, Kenji Omura provides incredibly tasteful, chorused guitar fills and a brief, lyrical solo. Notice how Omura uses volume swells and sliding double-stops to create a sense of floating. The chords are rich with minor 9ths and major 9ths, providing a sophisticated harmonic cushion.

#### 12. FANTASY — Meiko Nakahara (*Friday Magic*, 1982)
*Listening Cue:* A high-energy floor filler. The rhythm guitar plays highly aggressive, syncopated 16th-note funk scratches. Pay attention to how the guitar shifts between muted single-note chugs and full-voiced dominant 9th chords during the chorus, pushing the tempo and adding a brilliant sense of urgency to the groove.

#### 13. 街のドルフィン - 2020 Remaster — Kingo Hamada (*midnight crusin'*, 1982)
*Listening Cue:* This track is a masterclass in sophisticated AOR (Adult Oriented Rock) guitar. Listen to the lush major 9th chords and the subtle, clean guitar fills that embellish the vocal phrases, resolving voice-leading lines downward. The guitar parts here act like vocal backups, filling the gaps between lines with sweet, jazz-inflected phrasing.

#### 14. LOVE TRIP — Takako Mamiya (*LOVE TRIP*, 1982)
*Listening Cue:* The smooth, jazzy guitar work here relies on sophisticated jazz standards chord voicings (major 7ths, minor 9ths, and half-diminished ii-V lines). Listen to how the guitar comps gently behind the vocals, acting as a soft, rhythmic cushion. It is a brilliant example of how to make complex jazz chords feel incredibly accessible and relaxing.

#### 15. Summer Breeze — PIPER (*Summer Breeze*, 1983)
*Listening Cue:* Extremely guitar-forward. Keisuke Yamamoto and his band create a wall of chorused, jangly guitars. Listen to the interlocking dual-guitar parts playing arpeggiated lines and clean funk scratches that create a shimmering, breezy summer soundscape. This is the ultimate example of "resort pop" guitar, where the guitar tone is literally dialed in to sound like sunlight reflecting off the ocean.

#### 16. WINDY SUMMER — Anri (*Timely!!*, 1983)
*Listening Cue:* Produced by Kadomatsu. The track features a relentless, high-speed funk-comping guitar track that drives the song forward. The guitar solo in the middle is a soaring, highly technical, compressed fusion solo that shreds tastefully over the changes, using fast pentatonic runs and fusion scale alterations.

#### 17. 黄昏のBAY CITY — Junko Yagami (*黄昏のBAY CITY*, 1983)
*Listening Cue:* Focus on the heavy, syncopated rock-funk guitar comping that locks in with the driving synth bass. During the choruses, the guitar adds bright, high-register double-stop stabs that highlight the syncopated vocal rhythm. The track demonstrates how to use the guitar to build dynamic tension in a high-energy dance track.

#### 18. Midnight Pretenders — Tomoko Aran (*Fuyu Kukan*, 1983)
*Listening Cue:* Listen to the iconic, slow-grooving electric guitar intro. The guitarist uses a heavy chorus and analog delay to create an expansive, spacious environment. The single-note fills are highly vocal, using deep string bends and slow, wide vibrato. This track shows how much emotion can be squeezed out of simple pentatonic phrases when combined with rich spatial effects.

#### 19. ゆめうつつ — Lamp (*Lamp Genso*, 2008)
*Listening Cue:* A modern masterpiece of Japanese sophisti-pop. Listen to the delicate, jazz-samba acoustic guitar strumming and the gorgeous electric guitar voice-leading. Lamp's use of major 9(11) and minor 11 chords is incredibly sophisticated, mirroring your vocal arranging sensibilities. The electric guitar lines act as a third vocal voice, weaving in and out of the male-female harmonies with absolute grace.

### Act IV: The Global Echoes & Modern Revival (2016–Present)

#### 20. Cheapest Flight — PREP (*Futures*, 2016)
*Listening Cue:* Focus on the modern, ultra-clean British take on the Yacht Rock/City Pop guitar. The guitar plays highly rhythmic, staccato chord stabs and a subtle, funky single-note line that acts as a countermelody to the vocals. The chord choices here are highly jazz-influenced, utilizing major 7th and minor 9th extensions to create a smooth, sophisticated chillhop vibe.

#### 21. My Jinji — Sunset Rollercoaster (*JINJI KIKKO*, 2016)
*Listening Cue:* This Taiwanese band's crowning achievement. The guitar work in the first half is full of lush, chorused 7th chords. The second half shifts into a massive, extended psych-synth-pop jam where the guitar plays a soaring, melodic clean solo with long delays, resembling an atmospheric violin line. Pay attention to how the guitarist shifts positions smoothly, maintaining a singing, lyrical quality.

#### 22. Lover Boy — Phum Viphurit (*Lover Boy*, 2018)
*Listening Cue:* A modern Southeast Asian indie-pop gem. The guitar riff is incredibly catchy, featuring bright, clean-toned, syncopated double-stops and hammer-on/pull-off work that borrows heavily from modern math rock dexterity while remaining fully melodic and accessible. This is a brilliant example of how to make high-dexterity guitar playing feel incredibly fun, light, and pop-friendly.

#### 23. Wonder — ADOY (*LOVE*, 2018)
*Listening Cue:* Korean dream-pop meets city-pop. The guitar work is heavily layered, combining ambient, delayed volume swells with a jangly, chorused rhythm guitar that plays sophisticated jazz extensions under a catchy indie melody. Listen to how the ambient swells fill the sonic space, acting like synth pads while maintaining the organic texture of a guitar.

#### 24. NEON — YUKIKA (*NEON*, 2019)
*Listening Cue:* A perfect modern recreation of the 1980s city pop aesthetic. Listen to the ultra-precise, chorused funk-scratching guitar that runs through the entire track, locking in with the brass hits and massive slap-bass groove. This track showcases the absolute peak of modern J-Pop and K-Pop studio guitar precision, where every single muted 16th-note scratch is perfectly locked to the grid.

#### 25. Loretta — Ginger Root (*City Slicker*, 2020)
*Listening Cue:* The pinnacle of modern bedroom city-pop/indie-funk. The guitar work is extremely dry and tight, playing snappy, staccato funk stabs and a lively, syncopated single-note line that dances around the prominent bass melody. Ginger Root strips away the heavy 80s chorus in favor of a dry, punchy, 70s-style funk tone, proving that the harmonic and rhythmic core of City Pop works in any sonic environment.

## What to Listen For Across the Whole Arc

* **The Evolution of the Clean Tone:** Notice how the guitar tone transitions from the warm, direct, slightly-saturated 70s West Coast tone (Shigeru Suzuki) to the heavily compressed, chorused, and delayed "resort-pop" shimmer of the 80s (Piper, Anri), and finally to the ultra-dry, tight, bedroom-funk tones of today (Ginger Root).
* **The Nile Rodgers Influence:** Track the thread of syncopated 16th-note funk rhythm scratching and double-stop stabs. It starts with Tsunehide Matsuki in 1977, reaches peak studio perfection with Mariya Takeuchi and Casiopea in the 80s, and is reborn in the modern work of Yukika and Ginger Root.
* **Complex Harmony Made Pop:** Pay attention to how secondary dominants and slash chords are used to create harmonic tension without disrupting the pop appeal. Notice how these complex chords (like $F/G$ resolving to $Cmaj7$) create a sense of forward momentum and emotional lifting.
* **The Fusion Crossover:** Watch the boundary between pop music and instrumental jazz-fusion blur. Tracks by Casiopea and Masayoshi Takanaka show that City Pop was born in the exact same studios and played by the exact same musicians who defined the legendary golden age of Japanese jazz-fusion.
