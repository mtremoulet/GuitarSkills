## What Makes Jazz "Easy" on Guitar

"Easy" in jazz has nothing to do with the improvisation — that part is always hard. It refers to the underlying architecture: how many distinct chord areas you have to track, how often they change, and whether the form repeats in a predictable loop. For a developing guitarist, a track with a simple form is one where you can lock in the harmonic skeleton within the first few listens and then start *hearing inside it* — noticing how the soloist dances around chord tones, how the bass announces each chord change, how the rhythm guitar (if there is one) voices those chords.

Think of it like a violin player approaching a new key: once you've internalized where the tonic and dominant live under your hand, you can stop consciously mapping and start *playing*. The goal here is the same. These forms are simple enough that your brain can stop tracking "where are we?" and start tracking "what are they *doing* with where we are?"

The three architectures in this playlist: the **12-bar blues** (I7–IV7–I7–V7–IV7–I7, twelve bars repeating), the **32-bar AABA standard** (an eight-bar phrase repeated, then a contrasting eight-bar bridge, then the A again), and the **short vamp** (two to four chords cycling, often modal). All three repeat without variation in the form itself, which is exactly what you want when you're building your ear — the form is the fixed axis around which the improvisation orbits.

From a theory standpoint you already know: the 12-bar blues is the I7–IV7–V7 world you understand from your sax vocabulary. AABA standards are built almost entirely on ii–V–I chains, which you know as the fundamental motion of functional harmony. Vamps are the simplest of all — often a single tonic and its dominant, like a drone that breathes. The challenge is getting your ear to track these *in real time*, in twelve keys, at jazz tempos, against a rhythm section.

## The Playlist: Perspective and Arc

This playlist is built in four stages, progressing from the most structurally transparent forms to the most stylistically varied. The first six tracks are all blues — the clearest possible form, and the one most forgiving of wrong notes (blue notes are often right). The next four are vamp and modal-adjacent: just a handful of chords, looping. Then a block of standards played by modern guitarists who keep the harmony clean and forward. Finally, a set of contemporary originals where simple forms are used in inventive but still trackable ways.

The emphasis throughout is guitar-led recordings in trio or small combo settings — situations where the guitarist is the main harmonic voice and you can actually hear the chord changes implied in the comping and the solo. No guitar buried in a brass section, no rhythm guitar as background texture. You should be able to sit with headphones and hear every major harmonic move the guitar makes.

## Track-by-Track Commentary

### Part 1: Blues at the Core

**1. Chitlins Con Carne — Kenny Burrell** *(Midnight Blue, 1963)*

F blues shuffle at a slow-medium groove tempo — this is the benchmark entry point. The form is completely naked: twelve bars, period. What Burrell does is masterclass in economy: he plays the blues scale over the I chord (F7), shifts his target notes up a fourth when the IV chord (Bb7) arrives (notice how the F becomes a phrase-landing note in bar 5), and resolves to C7 in bar 9 like a complete sentence. Count along on your first listen — you'll lock in the 12-bar almost immediately. The saxophone's soulful response makes the chord boundaries even clearer.

**2. Midnight Blue — Kenny Burrell** *(Midnight Blue, 1963)*

Same album, slower tempo, even more exposed. Where "Chitlins" is a shuffle groove, "Midnight Blue" is a slow drag — every chord hangs in the air long enough to really feel it. Pay attention to bars 5–6 (the IV7 move) and bars 9–10 (the V7–IV7 turnaround): these are the two moments that define whether a blues sounds "right" or clunky. Burrell nails both with almost nothing — a few bent notes, a lot of space. This is your model for what "less is more" means in the blues.

**3. Idle Moments — Grant Green** *(Idle Moments, 1963)*

A heads-up: this is a 14-minute track, the longest on the list. Don't skip it. "Idle Moments" is a slow Bb blues with a 20-bar theme (a few extra bars added to let the melody breathe), but the solo sections revert to a clean 12-bar. Grant Green's guitar sits completely exposed in the texture — no piano in this recording. You hear every note he plays, including all the space between them. Green was the most blues-literal of the hard bop guitarists: his lines connect directly to the B.B. King and T-Bone Walker vocabulary, just filtered through a jazz sense of rhythm and chromatic approach. Track how he stays in the Bb blues scale almost exclusively over the I chord but adds a major 3rd (D natural) over the IV chord (Eb7) — that's the fundamental blues/bebop synthesis, right there.

**4. Full House — Wes Montgomery** *(Full House, 1962)*

F blues at medium-up swing tempo, live at the Tsubo club in Berkeley. Wes's famous three-stage approach — single-note lines, then octaves, then chord solos — makes the 12-bar form easy to track because each stage has a different textural character. Listen for the octave playing (the second stage): it's unmistakably Wes, a technique that came from not wanting to wake his children at night by practicing with a pick. The band announces every chord change like a clock ticking. By bar 9 you should be anticipating the V7 (C7) before it arrives.

**5. Bumpin' on Sunset — Wes Montgomery** *(Tequila, 1966)*

Slower and lusher — this is Wes in his later, more pop-influenced period, with strings. Don't be put off by the production; the guitar lines are clear as ever, and the form underneath is a simple 12-bar blues vamp at a slow grooving tempo. Notice how the changes breathe much more slowly here than on "Full House" — the harmonic rhythm is two beats per chord in some bars, four in others. This teaches your ear to follow the *feel* of a chord change rather than counting mechanically.

**6. Segment — Kurt Rosenwinkel** *(Intuit, 1999)*

Charlie Parker's "Segment" is a bebop 12-bar blues — same form you've heard in tracks 1–5, now played at a faster bebop tempo with a modern 1990s guitar trio sound. Rosenwinkel is the crucial bridge between the Blue Note era and the contemporary jazz guitar scene. His phrasing comes from Parker and Coltrane but runs through a warm, slightly distorted guitar voice. Use this track to ask: how do the harmonic landmarks feel at a faster tempo? The bars go by twice as fast, but the I–IV–V skeleton is still there. Listen for his approach to the turnaround (bars 11–12) — he often plays a iii–VI–ii–V sequence that sets up the top of the form.

### Part 2: Vamps and Modal Territories

**7. Breezin' — George Benson** *(Breezin', 1976)*

Leon Russell's composition, famously covered by Benson in a recording that went platinum — rare for a jazz guitar album. The form is not quite a blues but very simple: essentially a ii–V–I cycle in G major that repeats, with a short bridge. There's almost no harmonic complexity; the entire track is about the *sound* and the phrasing. Benson's tone here — warm, round, with that slight guttural quality — is the most imitated jazz guitar sound in popular music. Focus on how he builds phrases: start with a simple motif, develop it over two or three bars, resolve it cleanly. The simplicity of the harmony makes his phrase construction completely audible.

**8. James — Pat Metheny Group** *(Offramp, 1982)*

D Dorian from first note to last — a pure modal vamp. D Dorian is D–E–F–G–A–B–C: the D natural minor scale with a raised 6th (B natural instead of Bb), giving it a slightly brighter, more hopeful feel than pure minor. The harmony barely moves: it's mostly a Dm7 tonic with occasional G7 pulls (the IV chord in Dorian). What this track teaches is how to hear *color* without changes: Metheny navigates the modal space by choosing which notes to emphasize above the Dm7, creating tension and release purely through melodic choice rather than harmonic motion. From your violin/fiddle background — this is modal improvisation, the same principle as playing in D Dorian on a tune with a drone.

**9. Nocturne — Julian Lage** *(Arclight, 2016)*

A short (3:19) quiet ballad built on a simple repeating minor progression — roughly Am–G–F–E, or a i–VII–VI–V rotation in A minor. Lage's guitar is completely exposed, playing a gentle arpeggiated figure that doubles as both melody and harmony. This is one of the best tracks on the list for noticing how a guitarist implies chord changes *without* a bass or piano: every harmonic move is in the guitar's voicings and the melody notes he chooses. Perfect for identifying chord changes by ear, because the guitar has to do all the work.

**10. Last Train Home — Gilad Hekselman** *(Homes, 2015)*

A modern groover built on a repeating minor vamp — think of it as a jazz version of a funk ostinato. Hekselman's guitar sits front and center, and the form is short enough to feel like a loop. Israeli-born, NYC-based, Hekselman has one of the most lyrical guitar voices in contemporary jazz: he phrases like a singer, with long arching lines that peak and resolve over two to four bars. Notice how he controls *where in the bar* he starts a phrase — often picking up from the "and" of beat four, which gives everything a slightly floating, late feel. That rhythmic placement is one of the most characteristic things about modern jazz phrasing.

### Part 3: Short Standards, Modern Players

**11. Autumn Leaves — Julian Lage Group** *(Gladwell, 2009)*

The most learnable standard progression in jazz: a descending ii–V–I chain that moves from Bb major to G minor over eight bars, then loops. The chord sequence is Cm7–F7–Bbmaj7–Ebmaj7–Am7b5–D7–Gm (II–V–I in Bb major, then ii–V–i in G minor). You can see the parallel motion — each ii–V–I just drops a fourth from the last. Lage plays it straight but with great rhythmic clarity. Count the eight-bar A section repeatedly until you can *feel* where Cm7 comes in without counting. That's the goal with any standard: internalize the form, not just understand it.

**12. Darn That Dream — Lage Lund** *(Standards, 2007)*

Lage Lund is one of the most lyrical modern mainstream guitarists — this Norwegian-American guitarist won the Monk Competition in 2005 and has a sound somewhere between Jim Hall's restraint and Kurt Rosenwinkel's harmonic curiosity. "Darn That Dream" is a 32-bar AABA ballad with changes that move through I–VI–II–V in C major. The tempo is slow enough that you can follow every harmonic move in real time. This is a masterclass in ballad phrasing: long, melodic statements with space between them, each phrase ending on a chord tone that lets the harmony ring.

**13. I'll Be Seeing You — Jonathan Kreisberg** *(Night Songs, 2009)*

An ABAC 32-bar standard — slightly different from AABA in that the last eight bars contrast with both the A and B sections, giving it a more open-ended feeling. The changes are simple (F major, ii–V–I gestures, a few secondary dominants), and Kreisberg's playing is luminous and unhurried. Kreisberg is one of the finest harmonic thinkers in contemporary jazz guitar, but "Night Songs" is his most accessible album — the romanticism of the material pulls him toward melody over complexity. Listen for how he uses silence: the rests in his phrases are as informative as the notes.

**14. Blue in Green — Jonathan Kreisberg** *(Night Songs, 2009)*

The Bill Evans/Miles Davis composition from "Kind of Blue" — a ten-bar form built primarily on minor chord areas that cycle with a slightly suspended quality (Gm–C7–Bbmaj7–A7sus4–Dm–Dbmaj7–Cm7–F7–Bbmaj7–A7b9). It's not a blues, despite the name; it's a through-composed form that doesn't resolve strongly, creating a floating, introspective character. Kreisberg's version is stripped down and beautiful. For ear training: notice that almost every chord is a minor seventh or dominant seventh — there are no major chords fighting for attention. This teaches you to hear the difference between minor color and dominant color as chord qualities.

**15. Four (Live) — Peter Bernstein** *(Live at Smalls, 2010)*

Miles Davis's "Four" is a 32-bar AABA hard bop vehicle — medium-up swing tempo, clearly defined ii–V–I changes in Eb major with a short bridge that moves to the IV (Ab major). Bernstein is one of the most harmonically grounded guitarists in hard bop: his lines are bebop-fluent but always swinging, never scholastic. The live recording at Smalls means you get the raw, unvarnished version — no studio gloss. Track the A section (eight bars): Ebmaj7 for two bars, then ii–V (Fm7–Bb7) for two bars, back to Ebmaj7 for two bars, then a ii–V–I resolution. It's a repeating blueprint. This is your hard bop AABA model.

**16. There Will Never Be Another You — Pasquale Grasso** *(Solo Standards, 2020)*

Pasquale Grasso plays solo unaccompanied guitar — no bass, no drums, no piano. Everything you hear is his guitar: melody, harmony, rhythm, bass line. "There Will Never Be Another You" is an AABA standard in Eb major with clean ii–V–I progressions that Grasso spells out completely in his chord-melody playing. This is the ultimate ear-training track for standard form: because there's no rhythm section to lean on, every chord change *has* to be articulated by the guitarist. Listen for how he walks bass notes on beats 1 and 3 while playing melody on top — that's the traditional chord-melody technique that comes directly from classical counterpoint, which you'll recognize from your musical background.

**17. Conception I — Kurt Rosenwinkel** *(Intuit, 1999)*

"Conception" is a rhythm changes head — built on the same 32-bar AABA harmonic skeleton as "I Got Rhythm" (Gershwin), the most recycled form in jazz. The A section is essentially I–VI–II–V repeating every two bars: Bb–G7–Cm7–F7, cycling around the circle of fifths. The bridge is eight bars of dominant chords descending by fourths: D7–G7–C7–F7. Once you know rhythm changes, you know the harmonic architecture of hundreds of bebop tunes. Rosenwinkel plays it at a loose trio medium swing. Notice how the harmony moves every two beats in the A section — that rapid chord rhythm is characteristic of bebop and is what makes rhythm changes feel so propulsive.

### Part 4: Modern Guitar-Forward Originals

**18. Word for Word — Julian Lage** *(View With a Room, 2022)*

A minor-key original built on a simple descending progression — Am–G–F–E type motion, but developed over a tight trio. Lage's guitar is right in your ear: clean, present, every note audible. Notice how he develops a short melodic motif at the top of each chorus and then varies it through each pass of the form. This is the most efficient way to understand jazz improvisation — as theme and variation, not as "playing scales over chords." The form is short enough that you can count the choruses and notice exactly where he is in the structure at any point.

**19. Squint — Julian Lage** *(Squint, 2021)*

Medium swing original on a short AABA form. "Squint" showcases Lage's complete command of the bebop vocabulary as filtered through his own voice — the phrases are built from bebop cells but assembled with a folk-like directness. At this tempo you can clearly hear the ii–V–I motion that drives the A section. It's a track for understanding how modern guitarists inherit bebop vocabulary without just playing bebop: the swinging eighth notes are there, the chromatic approaches are there, but the phrasing shape is uniquely contemporary.

**20. Flower — Gilad Hekselman** *(Hearts Wide Open, 2011)*

A short, lyrical original on a straightforward AABA structure. Mark Turner's saxophone and Hekselman's guitar intertwine in a way that makes the guitar's harmonic role very clear: he's both voicing chords and playing melodic fills simultaneously. The tempo is gentle, the changes move slowly, and the guitar tone is warm and direct. A good track for hearing how a modern guitar comps in a conversation with a horn — it's not strumming full chords on every beat, it's punctuation, response, and texture.

**21. Lover — Nir Felder** *(Golden Age, 2014)*

Rodgers & Hart's "Lover" is an AABA 32-bar waltz in 3/4 time — the most guitaristically interesting meter because it's uneven in a way that throws off automatic strumming patterns. Felder plays it with a modern electric guitar sound and a loose trio feel, keeping the form completely legible while adding contemporary harmonic color. The 3/4 meter means the ii–V–I resolutions feel different rhythmically than in 4/4 — more spacious, more circular. From your broad rhythmic background, you'll find the waltz form very singable once you've heard it twice.

**22. Since You Asked — John Scofield** *(Time on My Hands, 1990)*

Scofield's playing sits at the intersection of jazz, blues, and rock — his guitar voice is immediately recognizable: slightly distorted, extremely funky in the phrase rhythms. "Since You Asked" is a medium blues-influenced groove with a simple form. Sco's rhythmic vocabulary is one of the most teachable in jazz guitar: he plays "behind the beat" in a very deliberate way, letting each phrase arrive just slightly late, which creates a looser, more conversational feel than the "on top of the beat" approach of bebop. Notice how his phrases feel like they're always *finishing* rather than *starting*.

**23. Have a Little Faith in Me — Bill Frisell** *(Have a Little Faith, 1992)*

A John Hiatt country-soul song rendered as a simple jazz guitar meditation. Frisell's guitar is completely alone at the center of the mix — the harmony is just I–IV–V in a major key, about as simple as harmony gets. The point of ending with this track is to hear what a master guitarist does with the *simplest possible harmonic material*: Frisell's tone, his vibrato, his choice of when to add a note versus sit in silence, his gentle rubato around the melody — all of this is pure guitar artistry applied to something a beginner could play the chords to. Simplicity is a destination, not a starting point.

## What to Listen For Across the Whole Arc

- **The IV chord moment in the blues**: In every 12-bar track, bar 5 is where the harmony shifts from I7 to IV7. Before you can hear anything else clearly, train your ear to anticipate that moment. It has a brightening, opening quality — like moving from tonic ground to a brief harmonic exhale.

- **Phrase endings on chord tones**: The great guitarists on this list almost always end their phrases on a note that belongs to the chord underneath. Listen for which note a phrase lands on — root, 3rd, 5th, or 7th — and how that landing note determines whether the phrase feels finished or suspended.

- **How guitar replaces the piano**: In piano-less trios (Green's "Idle Moments," Grasso's solo pieces, several of the Lage tracks), the guitarist has to do everything: melody, harmony, bass implied. Notice how their left hand voicings carry the harmonic information that a pianist would handle, and how that changes what the melodic lines do.

- **Rhythmic placement — where in the beat phrases start**: Modern jazz guitarists (Lage, Hekselman, Kreisberg) almost never start phrases exactly on beat 1. They typically begin on the "and" of 4, or the "and" of 2. That rhythmic offset creates the swinging, conversational quality that distinguishes jazz phrasing from classical phrasing. Compare to Metheny's "James" — his phrases are longer and more metrically regular, which is part of why that track sounds more lyrical and less "inside" the swing.

- **The turnaround**: In both blues and AABA standards, the last two bars of each chorus contain a "turnaround" — a ii–V (or I–VI–ii–V) that cycles back to the top. Listen for when the harmony gets busier or more chromatic in bar 11–12 of a blues, or bar 7–8 of an A section. That's the turnaround. Once you can hear it, you can feel the form breathing.
