## What Funk Lead Guitar Actually Is

Most discussion of funk guitar fixates on rhythm: the chopped sixteenth-note chords, the muted percussive scratches, the locked-in-with-the-drums thing Nile Rodgers perfected. That's for another playlist. This one is about the other tradition — the guitarist as soloist, melodist, and emotional storyteller within a funk context. It is a smaller, stranger lineage, and it is arguably the more expressive one.

The defining mechanic is voice imitation. Where a jazz guitarist might model their phrasing on bebop horn lines, the funk lead guitarist models their phrasing on the human voice — specifically on the Southern soul vocal tradition. Curtis Mayfield's guitar sounds like his own falsetto; Ernie Isley's wah-drenched leads are essentially horn-section fills with strings attached. The wah pedal is the primary tool because it mimics the vowel-shift of the human voice: rocking it forward is opening the mouth, pulling back closes it. When you hear a guitarist "talk" through a wah, they are literally singing through the instrument. As a saxophonist, you already know what it feels like when a phrase *breathes* — funk lead guitar is that same logic, just through a transducer instead of a reed.

The rhythmic placement is what separates funk lead from blues or rock lead. A blues guitarist bends into the beat; a funk guitarist bends *around* the beat. Phrases start on upbeats and resolve on unexpected downbeats, or they start on downbeats and end mid-bar in a way that creates forward momentum. The rest — the silence between phrases — is as load-bearing as the notes. This is the same economy you'd apply as a horn player: you don't hold a note because you can; you hold it because the phrase demands it, and then you stop. Funkiness lives in the stopping.

Harmonically, funk lead guitar is surprisingly lean. The core vocabulary is the minor pentatonic scale (your five-note blues box), extended with the flat five (the blue note) and occasional Dorian-mode passing tones. The sophistication comes entirely from *where* in the bar you place those notes and *how* you articulate them — with the wah, with the envelope filter, with a hard attack or a lazy pull-off. The melodic language is closer to a trumpet player's ethos than a classical violinist's: motifs, repetition with variation, call-and-response. If you've arranged an a cappella chart where the baritone line punches between bass and tenor phrases, you already understand the spatial logic of funk guitar lead playing.

## The Playlist: Perspective and Arc

This playlist follows the melodic-lead tradition from its psychedelic-soul origins in 1969 through five distinct phases: the Hendrix-inflected psychedelic funk of Funkadelic and the Isleys; the jazz-funk refinement of the mid-to-late '70s; Prince's synthesis of everything into something entirely personal; the neo-soul bridge of the late '90s and early 2000s; and the contemporary renaissance, where players like Nai Palm, Mark Lettieri, and Tom Misch are writing new chapters. The emphasis throughout is on tracks where the guitar is the *lead voice* — soloing, carrying the melody, or occupying the space a horn solo would fill. Rhythm guitar, however foundational, is not the subject here.

## Track-by-Track Commentary

### Part I: Psychedelic Soul + Early Funk (1969–1975)

**1. Cissy Strut — The Meters** *(The Meters, 1969)*

Leo Nocentelli's opening statement for this playlist is a study in restraint. He plays almost nothing — but what he plays is perfectly placed. Listen for the single-note descending lick that punctuates the horn groove around the 0:45 mark: five notes, delivered with a slightly lazy attack that lands just behind the beat, creating the sensation that the whole track leans. This is the first principle of funk lead guitar: occupy exactly the space you need and not a note more. Nocentelli's guitar is in constant call-and-response with Art Neville's organ, and neither one steps on the other. For a musician with a chamber ensemble background, think of it as two voices in careful counterpoint — except the counterpoint is in service of a groove, not a voice-leading exercise.

**2. Move On Up — Curtis Mayfield** *(Keep on Keeping On: Curtis Mayfield Studio Albums 1970–1974, 2019 Remaster)*

Use the full 8:53 remaster, not the single edit. The reason is that Mayfield's guitar doesn't fully emerge until the piece has breathed for a few minutes. He plays a detuned open-E guitar (tuned down a half step) in a distinctively choppy, falsetto-like single-note style that directly mirrors his vocal register. Around the 3:00 mark, the horn section drops away and the guitar takes over the melodic lead — notice how the phrasing is identical to what his voice would do: short exclamations, space, another short exclamation, resolution. He is playing his own vocal part. The wah is subtle here, mostly used for tonal color rather than dramatic swells. This is the root of the "guitar as voice" tradition.

**3. Maggot Brain — Funkadelic** *(Maggot Brain, 1971)*

The centerpiece of this entire playlist. George Clinton allegedly told Eddie Hazel to "play the first part like your mama just told you she's dying, and the second part like you found out it was a lie." The result is a ten-minute solo with no chord changes underneath — just a single root drone — that contains more emotional information per bar than most guitarists pack into a career. Harmonically, Hazel stays almost entirely in the minor pentatonic, but his use of vibrato (extremely wide, slow, and deliberate — closer to a violinist's than a rock guitarist's), his bend targets (he reaches for the major 3rd from below, landing somewhere between the minor and major third — the blue zone), and his phrasing arc (beginning sparse, building to overwhelming density, then retreating to silence) make this the definitive statement of the guitar-as-voice principle. This is Jimi Hendrix's psychedelic language translated into pure grief.

**4. Strawberry Letter 23 — Shuggie Otis** *(Freedom Flight, 1971)*

Before the Brothers Johnson covered this in 1977 and made it a pop hit, Shuggie Otis recorded the original at 17 years old in his home studio in Hollywood. His guitar here is layered and dreamy — he plays with a fingerpicked, almost harp-like delicacy that contrasts sharply with the Maggot Brain intensity preceding it. Listen for the descending melodic run around 0:30 that feels like it's slipping between the cracks of the rhythm — it's in the right time signature, but it floats above the grid. Shuggie was building toward something that wouldn't fully arrive until his 1974 masterpiece; the playing here is precocious and slightly impressionistic, pointing toward a future where funk guitar would absorb ambient and psychedelic textures.

**5. That Lady, Pts. 1 & 2 — The Isley Brothers** *(3+3, 1973)*

Ernie Isley heard Jimi Hendrix in 1969 — Hendrix was briefly in the Isley Brothers' touring band before he became famous — and spent the next decade processing that encounter into something distinctly his own. "That Lady" is the arrival: the wah-soaked, sustain-drenched lead guitar that opens the track and runs through it entirely is the most explicitly "singing" lead guitar in the canon. Ernie plays in a call-and-response dialogue with the vocals — he fills the exact spaces between vocal phrases, then trades off when the vocals drop out. Notice that he almost never plays a lick exactly the same way twice; this is improvisational variation in real time, not riff repetition. For a violinist: the vibrato Ernie uses is actually bow-technique vibrato — arm-based, wide, and used for expressiveness rather than tuning correction. It transforms the guitar's natural sustain into something vocal.

**6. Freddie's Dead — Curtis Mayfield** *(Superfly, 1972)*

The wah guitar on "Freddie's Dead" is Mayfield's most overt use of the effect as a rhythmic tool rather than just a tone-shaper. He opens the song with the guitar playing the hook — not the vocals, the guitar — and the wah rocks in a consistent 16th-note pattern that is, functionally, a second percussive voice underneath the kit. But within that rhythmic framework, the melodic content of the hook is genuinely vocal: a phrase that rises a major 3rd, rests, then descends back through the minor pentatonic. The "resting" moment — the silence in bar 2 of the hook — is where the funk lives. Compare this to "Move On Up": both tracks show Mayfield treating the guitar as his lead voice, but here the arrangement is sparser, making his guitar's role more obviously primary.

### Part II: Jazz-Funk Refinement (1974–1982)

**7. Inspiration Information — Shuggie Otis** *(Inspiration Information/Wings of Love, 1974)*

The album Shuggie Otis made almost entirely alone in his home studio, building every part layer by layer. His guitar work here is more assured than on "Strawberry Letter 23" — the lead lines have a settled, unhurried quality, like he knows exactly what he wants to say and isn't in a hurry to say it. The track is proto-everything: you can hear elements of future ambient music, quiet storm R&B, and even lo-fi in the production. The guitar melody in the opening section plays a minor pentatonic figure against a drum loop that Shuggie programmed himself. It is 1974; this approach would not be common for another twenty years. The lead tone is dry and warm, almost like an acoustic guitar with a touch of overdrive — completely different from the wah-heavy leads of his contemporaries.

**8. Fight the Power, Pts. 1 & 2 — The Isley Brothers** *(The Heat Is On, 1975)*

Ernie Isley at full power. The guitar on "Fight the Power" is less about singing and more about raw energy — he plays the opening riff on the upbeat and lets it hang in the air before the bass enters. The solo section (around the 2:30 mark) is Ernie at his most Hendrix-indebted: bends that go a full step and a half, screaming high-register vibrato, phrases that start in one rhythmic grid and land in another. But what keeps it firmly in the funk tradition rather than the rock tradition is the way the solo interacts with the groove: he never abandons the pocket. Even at peak intensity, his phrases resolve on beat 1 or the "and" of 4 — always landing somewhere that reinforces the cycle.

**9. Breezin' — George Benson** *(Breezin', 1976)*

The commercial jazz-funk crossover, and a case study in how jazz guitar vocabulary sounds when placed in a groove context. Benson's tone is cleaner and rounder than anything in the first section — less wah, more pure pickup-through-clean-amp warmth — and his phrasing is from the bebop tradition: chromatic passing tones, ii-V resolutions, runs that outline chord tones before resolving. What makes this "funk" rather than pure jazz is the rhythmic placement: even his most bebop-inflected runs land on groove-friendly positions. Benson famously hums along with his guitar solos in unison, which creates a subtle chorus effect. As a violinist and a cappella arranger, you may find his pitch certainty striking — every bent note has a clear target, and he hits it.

**10. Captain Fingers — Lee Ritenour** *(Captain Fingers, 1977)*

The most technically demanding track in the first half of the playlist. Ritenour plays a chord-melody style that sits between lead guitar and comping — he voices chords high on the neck and leads with the top notes while filling in harmony underneath. The title track demonstrates his approach: a smooth, almost neo-classical legato style applied to funk-adjacent grooves. Listen to how he articulates the line around 1:30 — hammer-ons and pull-offs create a seamless legato that sounds more like a violin than a pick attack. For someone with a violin background, this phrasing logic will be immediately recognizable. As a guitar technique goal, the legato fluency Ritenour demonstrates here is a significant milestone: it requires left-hand independence that takes months to develop.

**11. Street Life — The Crusaders** *(Street Life, 1979)*

Larry Carlton's guitar solo on "Street Life" is arguably the most elegant funk-lead moment on this playlist. He enters around the 5:00 mark after Randy Crawford's vocal, and what he plays is a clinic in phrasing economy: eight-bar solo, maybe twelve distinct notes, absolutely no wasted motion. Every note lands with precise conviction on a rhythmically charged position — he targets the "and" of 2, holds it, then drops a descending phrase that resolves squarely on 1 of the next bar. The tone is a semi-hollow with light overdrive, giving just enough sustain for his signature bends to bloom. Carlton demonstrates that you do not need complexity to say something powerful; you need the right note in the right place. Carlton would go on to be one of the most-recorded session guitarists of the '80s, and this solo is the reason why.

**12. One Nation Under a Groove — Funkadelic** *(One Nation Under a Groove, 1978)*

By 1978, Funkadelic had moved from the psychedelic extremism of "Maggot Brain" toward something more ecstatic and communal. The guitar interplay here involves multiple players — primarily Garry Shider and Michael Hampton — trading and layering leads over an extended groove. Listen for the way the lead guitar functions like a horn section: punchy, rhythmically precise phrases that interlock with the bass and drums rather than hovering above them. The shift from Eddie Hazel's solo-voice approach to this more collective, percussion-integrated guitar language is significant. The funk has gotten deeper, and the guitar has learned to lock into the pocket rather than float above it.

### Part III: The Prince Synthesis (1984–1986)

**13. Let's Go Crazy — Prince** *(Purple Rain, 1984)*

Prince is one of the most underrated guitar players in rock and funk history, perpetually overshadowed by his own genius at songwriting, production, and performance. "Let's Go Crazy" is a showcase, and the guitar solo that closes the album version is a decisive statement: he starts in the blues-rock tradition, works through a phrase structure that acknowledges Hendrix, then accelerates into something percussive and almost frantic before landing on a sustained wail. What makes it "Prince" rather than just a generic blues-rock solo is the phrase architecture — he builds in miniature arcs, each one a question-and-answer pair, before connecting them into the larger statement. The tone is bright and slightly dirty, a strat-style pickup through what sounds like a light crunch — cutting, present, aggressive.

**14. Purple Rain — Prince** *(Purple Rain, 1984)*

The opposite emotional register from "Let's Go Crazy." The "Purple Rain" guitar solo is one of the great sustained melodic statements in any genre — six minutes of song reaches its climax in a guitar voice that is recognizably human, recognizably grieving, and formally perfect. Prince uses wide vibrato (again, the arm-based violin style), controlled feedback, and slow bends that hang in the air before resolving. He never plays fast; he plays deliberately and inevitably. For someone with a violin background: the way Prince manages long tones here — adding vibrato late after the initial attack, controlling the rate of oscillation — is exactly correct bow technique translated to guitar. The note speaks first, then the vibrato gives it voice. Compared to "Maggot Brain," the psychedelic chaos has been replaced with formal control. This is the blues ballad tradition meeting the gospel tradition meeting the singer-songwriter tradition, unified by a guitarist who learned from all of them.

**15. Crazay — Jesse Johnson feat. Sly Stone** *(Shockadelica, 1986)*

Jesse Johnson was Prince's rhythm guitarist in The Time, and "Crazay" is his attempt to claim the funk lead guitar throne in his own right. The guitar tone is cleaner and more envelope-filtered than Prince's — listen for the auto-wah effect that shapes the attack of each note, giving phrases a percussive "quack" character. Johnson's leads are tighter and more rhythmically precise than Prince's, less given to extended lyricism, more interested in interlocking with the groove. The track features Sly Stone on vocals, which is a direct lineage signal: Johnson understands that he's working in the tradition of Sly & the Family Stone's guitarists, and he's updating it with mid-'80s production. The bridge section around 2:00 features a guitar break where Johnson plays entirely through an envelope filter with no other effect — this is the clearest demonstration in the playlist of what the auto-wah does to articulation.

### Part IV: The Neo-Soul Bridge (1997–2000)

**16. A Go Go — John Scofield** *(A Go Go, 1997)*

The moment where jazz guitar and funk merged completely, with Medeski Martin & Wood providing the swampy, organ-trio backdrop. Scofield has always played with a slightly gritty, twangy tone (he uses a lot of pick noise intentionally), and on "A Go Go" that rawness serves the funk perfectly. His phrasing is jazz-derived — motivic development, chromatic approach notes, phrase answers that turn back on themselves — but the time feel is deep-pocket funk. Listen for how he plays *behind* the beat: where Ernie Isley plays on or slightly ahead of the beat for intensity, Scofield pulls back, creating a loping, relaxed feel that is its own kind of authority. This album is one of the great crossover records in jazz-funk history, and this title track is its thesis statement.

**17. Chicken Grease — D'Angelo** *(Voodoo, 2000)*

The *Voodoo* album is one of the great funk records of the 21st century, a deliberate reclamation of the organic, live-feeling funk of Sly Stone and Parliament-Funkadelic from the synthesizer-dominated '80s and '90s. "Chicken Grease" is its most guitar-forward track: a hypnotic, repeated vamp with a wah-filtered single-note guitar hook that is as much rhythm as it is melody. The recording credit for guitar varies depending on the source, but the playing is clearly indebted to Ernie Isley and the Isley Brothers tradition — the same percussive wah articulation, the same call-and-response logic with the bass. Where "Maggot Brain" extended the guitar into pure expression, "Chicken Grease" brings it back to the pocket. This is the document that told a generation of neo-soul musicians that organic funk was still the most powerful thing in the room.

### Part V: The Modern Renaissance (2012–2018)

**18. Nakamarra — Hiatus Kaiyote feat. Q-Tip** *(Tawk Tomahawk, 2012)*

Melbourne's Hiatus Kaiyote, led by singer-guitarist Nai Palm, arrived in 2012 as something genuinely new: a band that synthesized neo-soul, jazz harmony, and progressive composition through a deeply funky rhythmic sensibility. Nai Palm's guitar playing is explicitly anti-pattern — she resists the convention of "play the pentatonic box" and instead moves harmonically, using major 7th chord voicings in a way that's closer to jazz guitar than funk. But the rhythmic feel of her lead phrasing is pure pocket. Listen for her guitar figure at 1:15 — it's a descending line built from the major scale over a minor chord, creating a Dorian tonality, but it arrives with the attack and precision of a funk lead. The Q-Tip feature deepens the connection to hip-hop's sampling of funk vocabulary. If you are a theory person: the harmonic language Nai Palm uses is the Dorian mode applied to funk, which is the jazz-blues synthesis that makes neo-soul distinct from either genre alone.

**19. Shofukan — Snarky Puppy** *(We Like It Here, 2014)*

Snarky Puppy is a collective, and "Shofukan" is a demonstration of what happens when you put multiple world-class funk and jazz players in a room and let them develop a groove over seven minutes. The guitar work — primarily Bob Lanzetti — sits in the pocket for most of the track before breaking into lead territory around the 3:30 mark. What's notable is how the lead guitar enters the conversation: it doesn't take over, it joins. The phrasing is in dialogue with the keyboard and the horn line simultaneously, creating a three-way call-and-response. This is the collective approach to funk guitar lead, evolved from Funkadelic's later multi-guitar approach, updated with the compositional rigor of the modern jazz scene. The time feel throughout is extraordinarily precise — these musicians are playing exactly in the groove, not approximating it.

**20. The Force — Lettuce** *(Crush, 2015)*

Lettuce is the jam-scene inheritor of the Meters' and Meters-adjacent tradition: a rhythm-section-forward band where the guitar (Adam Smirnoff) is in service of the groove rather than floating above it. "The Force" is Lettuce at their most economical: a single repeated figure that evolves through the track with slight variations. Smirnoff's lead tone is cleaner and brighter than the vintage players — he's working with modern pickups through a cleaner amp chain — but his rhythmic placement is firmly in the Nocentelli tradition. Listen for the moments where he breaks from the figure into a brief melodic statement: they happen exactly where you'd expect a horn player to breathe, and they resolve exactly where you'd expect a phrase to land. This is learned, cultivated, deliberately historical vocabulary.

**21. Come Down — Anderson .Paak** *(Malibu, 2016)*

The guitar on "Come Down" is primarily Jeff Gitelman (Paak's key collaborator on *Malibu*), and it demonstrates how the funk lead tradition has been absorbed into contemporary R&B production without being credited. The wah-filtered guitar figure that runs through the track is straight out of the Curtis Mayfield playbook — rhythmically identical to the pattern on "Freddie's Dead" — but the production context is hip-hop influenced, with sampler-style drum programming underneath. Listen for how the guitar becomes more aggressive in the chorus: the filter opens wider, the attack gets harder, and the phrase lands more decisively on the downbeat. Anderson .Paak bridges James Brown's funk, Prince's eclecticism, and modern trap production in a single track, and the guitar is the element that connects him most directly to the historical lineage.

**22. Disco Yes — Tom Misch feat. Poppy Ajudha** *(Geography, 2018)*

Tom Misch emerged from South London's jazz-influenced bedroom-production scene, and "Disco Yes" is his clearest statement of funk guitar as melodic voice. His playing is clean and warm, indebted to George Benson's jazz-funk vocabulary but inflected with the understated aesthetic of the contemporary electronic-adjacent scene. The guitar solo around 2:00 is the track's emotional peak: a smooth, legato line in the minor pentatonic that borrows from the jazz guitar tradition without quoting it directly. Misch's phrasing is more patient than most of his contemporaries — he sets up phrases with space, allows them to breathe, and resolves them without rushing. For a listener coming from the jazz tradition, this will feel familiar; for someone approaching from the funk side, it represents the most refined and least percussive point on the spectrum.

**23. Swagism — Ghost-Note feat. Nigel Hall** *(Swagism, 2018)*

Ghost-Note, led by drummer Robert "Sput" Searight and percussionist Nate Werth (both of Snarky Puppy), is perhaps the most hardcore funk outfit of the current era. "Swagism" is the one track on this playlist where you can hear the entire history played forward simultaneously: the wah-envelope filter lead guitar is in direct dialogue with the Meters and Funkadelic tradition, the production is modern and crisp, and Nigel Hall's vocal is pure soul. The guitar work (primarily Cody Blackbird in the Ghost-Note live setup) is aggressive and rhythmically tight — less lyricism, more percussion. This is where funk guitar lead arrives in the present: it has reabsorbed the historical vocabulary, filtered it through contemporary production, and arrived at something that is recognizably connected to "Cissy Strut" and "Maggot Brain" while sounding completely contemporary.

## What to Listen For Across the Whole Arc

- **The wah pedal as vowel generator.** Track how the wah transitions from a stylistic choice in 1969 (Nocentelli barely uses it) to a primary articulation tool (Mayfield, Isley, Hazel) to a taken-for-granted color (Scofield's gritty tone, modern envelope filters). The effect is the same across six decades; only the subtlety of its application changes.

- **The silence between phrases.** Every guitarist in this playlist uses rests as structural elements, not empty space. Try counting the beats of silence between Hazel's phrases in "Maggot Brain" — they're as carefully placed as the notes. When you reach the modern tracks, notice that the same logic applies: Tom Misch's patience, Mark Lettieri's economy.

- **The arc from maximalism to economy.** The playlist moves from Hazel's ten-minute psychedelic solo toward Carlton's twelve-note statement on "Street Life." This is not a decline; it's the tradition learning to say more with less. Prince synthesizes both extremes — maximalism when needed, restraint when called for.

- **Call-and-response geography.** Listen for how each guitarist positions their phrases relative to the other instruments. In the early tracks, the guitar responds to the horn section. By the middle of the playlist, the guitar *is* the horn section. By the modern tracks (Snarky Puppy, Ghost-Note), the guitar is part of a conversation that involves keyboard, percussion, and production elements simultaneously.

- **Vibrato as vocabulary.** Wide, slow vibrato (Hazel, Ernie Isley, Prince) signals emotional intensity and is drawn from the blues and gospel traditions. Narrow, quick vibrato (Benson, Ritenour, Scofield) signals jazz refinement. Nai Palm uses almost no vibrato — her expressiveness comes from harmonic choice instead. The vibrato an artist chooses tells you exactly where they're coming from.
