## What the Modern Scene Actually Is

To understand the sound of the modern (2021–2026) ambient neo-soul scene—often centered around the South London jazz-soul renaissance but stretching globally—it helps to contrast it directly with the traditional jazz, soul, blues, and funk you already play. While this music shares historical DNA with those genres, it "holds" its key musical elements—rhythm, harmony, phrasing, and tone—in a fundamentally different way. 

### 1. Rhythm: "Dilla Time" vs. Active Syncopation
Traditional funk and soul are built on a razor-sharp, driving grid. In a classic funk band, the groove is highly syncopated and on-the-grid, with active rhythm guitar parts (like Nile Rodgers' snappy Strat scratch) locking in precisely with the kick and snare. Traditional jazz relies on the forward-propelling swing of triplets and walking basslines to carry a horizontal drive. 

Modern ambient neo-soul, by contrast, operates on **"Dilla Time" or "lazy swing."** Pioneered by producer J Dilla and bassists like Pino Palladino, this feel relies on micro-timing variations where the snare is intentionally delayed behind the absolute grid, while the hi-hats might stay straight. The pocket is not driving; it is *hypnotic* and *laid back*. 
*   **How to hold it differently:** You must relax your time-keeping hand completely. Instead of snapping hard on the subdivisions, let your pick drag slowly through the strings, creating a slight "spongy" lag. Play right on the tail-edge of the beat.

### 2. Harmony: Loops of Stasis vs. Functional Journeys
Traditional jazz and blues are functional and horizontal. A jazz standard moves through a series of tension-and-resolution blocks—constantly navigating $II-V-I$ progressions, secondary dominants, and modulations to lead the listener on a harmonic journey. 

Modern ambient neo-soul is built on **non-functional loop stasis**. The entire song is typically built around a repeating 2- or 4-bar chord loop. The chords themselves are highly complex and extended ($maj9$, $m11$, $maj13$, $9sus4$), but they do not resolve in a traditional way. They hang in the air, spinning in a beautiful, circular orbit. 
*   **How to hold it differently:** Drawing on your a cappella arranging background, think of these loops as a static harmonic landscape rather than a path. The movement doesn't come from chord changes, but from **internal voice leading**. Keep the outer chord shapes static while moving single inner voices (e.g., suspending and releasing a 4th, or moving a 9th to a 3rd) to create shifting colors within the same loop.

### 3. Phrasing: Whispering Liquid vs. Speaking or Shouting
In blues and rock, lead guitar is an active, vocal shout—using aggressive string bends, digging pick attacks, and wide finger vibrato to project emotion. In jazz bebop, phrasing is scalar and horn-like, outlining changing chords with arpeggios and chromatic enclosures.

Modern neo-soul phrasing is **whispering, liquid, and highly legato**. It mimics modern R&B vocal inflections—breathy, intimate, and filled with micro-embellishments. Lead lines rely on slow slides, hammer-ons from open strings, delicate pull-offs, double-stops, and harp-like chord rolls.
*   **How to hold it differently:** Shift away from your violin position-shifting and bebop scalar patterns. Think of the neck as a smooth, fretless canvas where notes melt into one another. Slide into chord tones from a half-step below with a slow, deliberate glissando, and roll chord voicings sequentially with your thumb rather than striking them all at once.

### 4. Tone: The Guitar as a Synthesizer Pad
While jazz, blues, and funk celebrate the dry, natural, acoustic-electric woodiness of the guitar, modern ambient neo-soul treats the guitar as an atmospheric synthesizer pad. 
*   **How to dial it in:**
    *   **Compression:** Run a compressor at the front of your chain with a relatively high ratio and fast attack. This squishes the initial pick transient (the "pluck") and boosts the decay, making your guitar feel soft, spongy, and Rhodes-like under the fingers.
    *   **Modulation:** Add a subtle chorus or vibrato to create a slight pitch wobble, mimicking the warm, nostalgic degradation of old tape machines or lo-fi hip-hop vinyl.
    *   **Ambient Wash:** Drench your signal in dark analog delays and lush plate or hall reverbs. This rounds off any remaining sharp edges of your notes, letting your chords melt into the background like a synth pad.

## The Playlist: Perspective and Arc

This playlist is curated to explore the modern neo-soul landscape from **2021 to 2026**. Rather than retracing the historical roots of the Soulquarians, this list focuses entirely on how the scene sounds *today*. 

The playlist is structured in three distinct acts:
1.  **Act I: The South London Core & Bedroom Beatmakers** (Tracks 1–8) — Anchored by the warm, highly melodic "Tom Misch" lineage, highlighting clean, bedroom-produced, fingerstyle-rich phrasing.
2.  **Act II: Ambient Textures, House Beats & Virtuosity** (Tracks 9–15) — Explores the crossover into deep-house/jazztronica and ambient-virtuoso territories, where the guitar functions as a textured pad.
3.  **Act III: Modern Groove, Soulful Phrasing & Deep Basslines** (Tracks 16–23) — Focuses on the deep pocket, showing how modern guitarists interact with modern bass heavyweights (like Rocco Palladino) and organic, dry neo-soul production.

## Track-by-Track Commentary

### Act I: The South London Core & Bedroom Beatmakers

**1. Insecure — Tom Misch** *(Single, 2024)*
A masterclass in modern melodic neo-soul guitar. Misch blends classic George Benson chord-solo phrasing with modern R&B vocal sensibilities. Notice the spongy compression on his clean Stratocaster, rounding off the pick attack. The rhythm guitar loop is a simple, highly-swung 2-bar progression that leaves wide open space for the vocal melody.

**2. Crescent Moon — Conor Albert** *(Collage 2, 2022)*
Conor Albert is one of the premier multi-instrumentalists carrying the Misch torch in South London. This track is built on a gorgeous, fingerstyle-rolled chord progression using $maj9$ and $m11$ shapes. Listen to how the acoustic guitar and the clean electric guitar double each other, creating a rich, organic-meets-electronic texture.

**3. Forget — Conor Albert** *(Collage 1, 2021)*
A slightly uptempo beat that showcases Conor's rhythm playing. The guitar here is dry, tucked back in the mix, and serves as the rhythmic engine of the track. Instead of playing heavy full chords, he plays tight, two-note "double-stops" (thirds and sevenths) that lock in perfectly with the offbeat hi-hats.

**4. Settle Down — Beau Diako feat. Rad Museum** *(Nylon, 2022)*
Beau Diako is the pioneer of modern "glitchy" neo-soul guitar. His style is characterized by nylon-string guitars processed through modern pitch-shifting and vibrato effects. Listen to the micro-slides and hammer-ons; it sounds like the guitar is breathing or crying. This is the ultimate example of liquid phrasing.

**5. Flamingo — Beau Diako** *(Nylon, 2022)*
An instrumental showcase of Beau's highly unique chord construction. He uses open strings combined with high-fretboard extensions, creating massive, harp-like voicings. Notice how the guitar is completely dry in the center but surrounded by a wide, shimmering ambient delay field on the sides.

**6. Pretty Face — Maya Delilah** *(It's Not You, It's Me, 2021)*
Maya Delilah is a phenomenal London-based guitarist who brings a playful, pop-sensitive approach to neo-soul. The intro riff is a masterclass in combining a bassline and a chordal melody simultaneously. Listen to her clean tone—it is bright and snappy, using the bridge and middle pickups of a Stratocaster to cut through the heavy, sub-bass-driven mix.

**7. Moonflower — Maya Delilah** *(Single, 2022)*
A gorgeous, slow-burning ballad. Maya's guitar solo here is exceptionally lyrical. Rather than playing fast scale runs, she uses slow, vocal-like slides and half-step bends that mimic a singer's voice. The phrasing is incredibly patient—she leaves entire beats empty, allowing the notes to ring out and decay naturally.

**8. Rain — Ruben Wan** *(Single, 2021)*
Ruben Wan is part of the global bedroom-guitar movement, known for highly polished, R&B-phrased instrumental tracks. "Rain" features incredibly precise fingerstyle execution. Notice the subtle "slap-harmonic" technique on the chords, giving the progression a rhythmic click that acts like a snare drum.

### Act II: Ambient Textures, House Beats & Virtuosity

**9. jazz is for ordinary people — berlioz** *(jazz is for ordinary people, 2023)*
Berlioz has taken the internet by storm by blending deep-house beats with ambient jazz sax and filtered neo-soul guitar. The guitar here is not a lead instrument; it is a warm, filtered rhythmic loop that is drenched in a low-pass filter. It represents how to play "rhythm guitar" in a house context—minimalist, warm, and highly repetitive.

**10. wash my sins away — berlioz** *(jazz is for ordinary people, 2023)*
Another berlioz masterpiece. The guitar chords here are extremely lush and jazz-inflected ($maj9$ to $m9$ changes), but they are washed in a massive, ambient hall reverb. The notes lose their distinct boundaries and melt into a beautiful, warm, atmospheric pad that floats beneath the saxophone solo.

**11. Way Out — FKJ** *(VINCENT, 2022)*
FKJ (French Kiwi Juice) is a master of looping and soundscapes. On "Way Out," the guitar is clean, heavily compressed, and played with a very soft touch. Listen to the way the delay repeats are timed to the tempo, creating an organic echo chamber that fills the stereo field.

**12. Us — FKJ** *(VINCENT, 2022)*
This track features a gorgeous, ambient guitar duet with himself. One guitar plays a dry, muted acoustic rhythmic figure, while a second electric guitar floats on top with a lush chorus and a long-tail reverb. It's a great study in how to layer two distinct guitar characters within an ambient mix.

**13. Rise — Mansur Brown** *(D2, 2023)*
Mansur Brown is the virtuoso of the South London ambient guitar scene. His tone is iconic: a clean electric guitar drenched in pitch-vibrato (giving it a constant wobble) and massive stereo delays. On "Rise," his playing is fast and incredibly articulate, yet it feels entirely ambient because the notes are cushioned by a heavy, trap-style sub-bass.

**14. Mashita — Mansur Brown** *(Heiwa, 2021)*
A dark, atmospheric masterpiece. The guitar plays a repeating, eastern-tinged minor loop. Mansur uses his guitar's volume pedal or knob to roll off the attack of the notes, creating "swells" that sound more like a synthesizer or a violin section than an electric guitar.

**15. Sensual Loving — Blue Lab Beats** *(Motherland Journey, 2022)*
Blue Lab Beats is the production powerhouse of the South London jazztronica scene. On this track, the guitar plays a tight, highly swung neo-soul rhythm part. The tone is heavily squished by a compressor, making the chords sound incredibly tight, uniform, and "sampled," even though they are played live.

### Act III: Modern Groove, Soulful Phrasing & Deep Basslines

**16. Tioga Pass — Yussef Dayes feat. Rocco Palladino** *(Black Classical Music, 2023)*
A driving, spiritual jazz-soul track. While Yussef's drumming is incredibly intense and active, Rocco Palladino's bass and the electric guitar keep the track grounded. The guitar plays a continuous, hypnotic arpeggiated loop, showing how a guitarist can build tension in an ambient track without ever playing loudly or changing the chord shape.

**17. Gelato — Yussef Dayes** *(Black Classical Music, 2023)*
A relaxed, sunny groove. The guitar tone here is warm and woody (likely a semi-hollow body guitar), utilizing beautiful, close-voiced jazz chords ($maj7$ to $min9$). Listen to the interaction between the guitar's chordal rolls and the laid-back, late snare hits of the drums.

**18. Groceries — CARRTOONS** *(Homegrown, 2022)*
CARRTOONS (Ben Carr) produces bass-led, organic bedroom soul. "Groceries" features a stunning, minimalist guitar part. The guitar plays brief, clean double-stops that fill the gaps between the vocal lines. It is a masterclass in economy of notes—never playing more than two notes at a time, yet filling the harmonic space perfectly.

**19. Cadillac — CARRTOONS feat. Justus West** *(Homegrown, 2022)*
Featuring modern neo-soul guitar standout Justus West. The guitar here is dry, organic, and incredibly funky, but held with a relaxed, modern pocket. Listen to the micro-slides and the way the guitar "chucks" (muted strums) are placed exactly in the pockets left by the bass.

**20. Sweet Isolation — Oscar Jerome** *(The Spoon, 2022)*
Oscar Jerome brings a darker, slightly alternative jazz-soul edge to the London scene. The guitar work here is raw and dry, using chord shapes with flat-fifths and sharp-ninths that introduce a slightly tense, modern jazz-fusion flavor to an otherwise lush, ambient landscape.

**21. Super Sad Always — Melanie Faye** *(Melanie Faye EP, 2020/2021)*
Melanie Faye is the internet-breaking guitarist who helped popularize the modern neo-soul chord-roll aesthetic. "Super Sad Always" is a solo-guitar-led track that is pure ear candy. Her thumb-picked basslines are warm and round, while her index and middle fingers execute rapid-fire chord rolls, hammer-ons, and harp-harmonics.

**22. Build Me Up — Cleo Sol** *(Mother, 2021)*
Cleo Sol and producer Inflo (SAULT) represent the organic, warm, spiritual side of the modern South London scene. The guitar here is an acoustic, dry, and incredibly intimate instrument. It doesn't use any modern modulation or heavy reverb—proving that "ambient" and "lush" can be achieved purely through warm, close-voiced chord choices and a gentle, fingerstyle touch.

**23. Flowers — Jordan Rakei** *(The Loop, 2024)*
The perfect resolution to the playlist. Jordan Rakei's lush vocal harmonies are supported by a beautiful, simple, clean electric guitar playing delicate major 7th and suspension shapes. The guitar tone is warm, clean, and bathed in a soft hall reverb, acting as the quiet, soulful anchor of a massive vocal arrangement.

## What to Listen For Across the Whole Arc

When listening to this playlist, track these three elements to see how they evolve across the different artists:

*   **The Attack of the Note (The Pick vs. The Finger):** Notice how rarely these guitarists use a hard, aggressive plectrum strike. Instead, listen for the soft, fleshy thumb stroke (Tom Misch, Conor Albert) or the seamless, hammer-on legato (Beau Diako, Mansur Brown). They are intentionally hiding the "pop" of the string to make the instrument sound more vocal and horn-like.
*   **The Modulated "Wobble" (Pitch Drift):** Compare the dry, organic acoustic tone of Cleo Sol's *Build Me Up* with the heavy, wobbly pitch-vibrato on Mansur Brown's *Rise*. This pitch wobble is the modern equivalent of a violinist's vibrato or a singer's vocal shake—used to make the clean electric guitar feel alive, nostalgic, and slightly synth-like.
*   **Double-Stops and Voicings of Economy:** Notice how these guitarists rarely play six-string barre chords. They almost exclusively play 3- or 4-note voicings, often stripping it down to simple 2-note "double-stops" (thirds and sevenths). This leaves massive acoustic space in the lower-middle register for the heavy basslines to breathe.
